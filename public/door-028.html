Returning to the Dvādaśānta Wherever You Are

Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra · dhāraṇā 28 · verse 51 (Jaideva Singh numbering)

Door 28Verse 51ordinary life · concentration · breath (in one reading)PracticeDraft · awaiting guardian review

During the day, briefly return attention to the space just above the head, again and again.

The verse

yathā tathā yatra tatra dvādaśānte manaḥ kṣipet | pratikṣaṇaṃ kṣīṇavṛtter vailakṣaṇyaṃ dinair bhavet ||

However and wherever one is, let one cast the mind into the dvādaśānta, moment after moment; as the mind's fluctuations wane, within days something extraordinary arises.

School paraphrase drawing on Jaideva Singh (1979), Christopher Wallis and Bettina Bäumer (as printed in Wallis's concordance); not a quotation.

Commentary

Singh: the mind may be fixed at any dvādaśānta: above the head, twelve finger-widths outside the nose at the end of the out-breath, or the inner centre. Śivopādhyāya glosses 'extraordinary' as the incomparable state of Bhairava. Wallis's concordance quotes Reps, who renders it 'in worldly activity, keep attentive between the two breaths' and notes it as Lakshmanjoo's favourite.

How to sit with it

During ordinary activity (walking, washing, waiting), briefly place attention at the dvādaśānta: just above the head, or at the end of the out-breath in front of the nose. Then carry on. Do it again and again, a moment at a time.

Where attention rests

The dvādaśānta, returned to briefly and often during the day

The mind's fluctuation thinning

Not a body shape. The map is one point returned to again and again through the day, whatever one is doing.

Breath

Natural breath. Count about four in and four out only if it helps you settle, then let the count go. No forced hold.

The counts are an optional school pacing aid, not part of the verse. Hold is always 0: this school never adds a deliberate breath-hold.

Any steady, comfortable position (school addition)

Any steady, comfortable position: sitting on a chair, sitting on the floor with support under the hips, or lying on the back. Standing or walking slowly is also acceptable when that is easier. Choose the position in which the body needs the least effort.

School addition. Verse 51 does not specify a body position. The seat is a convenience so the dharana can be practised; it is not part of the technique and makes no claim of tradition.

Safety

  • Pain is information. Pause, regress, or exit. Never push through sharp pain.
  • General practice caution only; not medical advice. Keep your main attention on anything that needs it, such as traffic or tools; this is a light return, not a withdrawal. If anything feels distressing (dizziness, agitation, strong emotion), stop, open the eyes and rest attention on the feet and the floor.
  • This door needs no particular posture: sit on a chair, sit on the floor with support, or lie down. Eyes may stay open.

The eight beats (spoken script)
  1. Name the door. This is Door 028: returning to the dvādaśānta wherever you are. Vijñāna Bhairava Tantra dharana 28, verse 51.
  2. Geometry. No shape to make. This one is practised in ordinary life. The map is one point, just above the head or at the end of the out-breath.
  3. Orientation. Eyes open. Keep doing what you are doing. Let a part of attention return to the point.
  4. Breath. Natural breath. Count about four in and four out only if it helps. No forced hold.
  5. Attention. Whatever you are doing, touch that point for a moment. Then carry on. Again and again through the day.
  6. Reaction cue. School image: repeated small returns wearing down restlessness. A metaphor, not medicine.
  7. Adiyogi thread. Verse 51: however and wherever you are, cast the mind into the dvādaśānta moment after moment; as fluctuation wanes, something extraordinary arises.
  8. Exit / integrate. Keep the habit. Next time you wash your hands or wait for a door, return to the point for one breath.
